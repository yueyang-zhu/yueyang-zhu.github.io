The Family Backgrounds of Song-Dynasty Jinshi across Time and Space

With Yu Hao

Journal of Peking University (Philosophy and Social Sciences), 62(2), 128–139 · 2025

Abstract

This paper builds a historical dataset covering 28284 jinshi from Song dynasty, 8134 with detailed family backgrounds. We define a jinshi with commoner backgrounds if his father and grandfather did not hold any official positions, and investigate the time trend and geographic pattern of commoner jinshi as share of all jinshi and commoner jinshi as share of population. Both indexes show long-run increases over the Song dynasty and concentration in the Southeast. In particular, commoner jinshi as share of all jinshi increases from 45% in the early North Song to 65% towards the end of Southern Song. Using cross-sectional data at prefecture level, we show a strong positive correlation between these social mobility measures and cheap access to books and private education. The correlations between social mobility and level commercialization or North-south migration, however, depend on how these variables are measured. Further studies require using variations in time and space to make causal identification.

The Logic and Consequences of Equalizing Bureaucratic Recruitment: Evidence from the 1073 Examination Reform in Song China

With Yu Hao and Erik H. Wang

Abstract

We study how equalizing opportunities for bureaucratic recruitment reshapes politics in distinct stages. Initially, it brings more officials from humble backgrounds into the bureaucracy. Their lack of patronage ties, however, limits prospects for career advancement and thus motivates them to seek promotion by displaying exceptional loyalty and zeal in carrying out the ruler's preferred policies, often with downstream effects on governance and social stability. We test this argument using a reform of the civil service examinations in Northern Song China (960-1127), which shifted from literary composition to formulaic interpretation of the Confucian classics, a change that favored candidates from modest backgrounds. The reform increased the presence of nonelite bureaucrats whose hometowns had greater access to printed books. These officials extracted more revenue as prefects, were increasingly assigned to posts implementing the ruler's agenda, pursued the ruler's preferred policies with greater intensity, and faced higher rates of rebellion.

Information Is Power: Legibility Reform, Fiscal Appropriation, and Public Goods Provision in Late Ming China

With Yu Hao

Abstract

Informational legibility is a cornerstone of state capacity, yet its expansion can empower an extractive state to optimize exploitation at the expense of social welfare. This paper investigates a fiscal legibility reform in late Ming dynasty, which improved the central government's knowledge of local fiscal information. Using a DID method, we find that after the reform, prefectures with greater retained tax revenues faced a significant drop in disaster relief. For mechanisms, evidences from heterogeneity of tax exemption, illegal local surtaxes (huohao), and predatory mining taxes reveal exaggerated central appropriation. In the end, local public goods provision collapsed and triggered more peasant uprisings. Fiscal legibility improvement transformed into less public goods provision and more fiscal expropriation, suggesting that the decentralization of fiscal information in late imperial China was a strategic equilibrium of the central-local interaction.
